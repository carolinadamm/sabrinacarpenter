Room 1 · Agreement

You're right. I said it. Savour it.

Exhibit A

I'll start with your side, because it's good, and because I'd rather lose an argument than win it badly.

Objection 01 / Choice

“Choosing” isn't the whole truth.

Choices happen inside a world that rewards some choices and punishes others. We are looked at before we get to decide how. Clothing, too, lives inside that.

Objection 02 / Representation

We need icons who don't all look the same.

The polished, thin, light-skinned, effortlessly “clean” ideal has been the default for a very long time, and defaults are not neutral.

Objection 03 / Complicity

You're hard on yourself for the small compromises.

The laugh at the joke, the mansplaining you let pass. Being hyper-aware of all this every single day is exhausting.

Objection 04 / Lived experience

It isn't only theory.

Pop culture's body imagery can land in a body that has lived through something. That is real.

Room 2 · Reframe

Matriarchy, a short definition.

One law
dictionary.exe—□×
ma·tri·ar·chy / noun

1. A made-up country. 2. Its only law: nobody gets ranked for what she likes.

Not patriarchy with the pronouns swapped. No throne, no ranking, no purity exam at the door. (Think Berghain, but everybody gets in and the music is also okay.)

A design thought experiment, not a historical claim.

The jar: not a debt, a tax.

About the laughing and the not-calling-out. Sara Ahmed writes about the “feminist killjoy”: the woman who says the thing and pays for it. When you laugh along, you're not failing feminism. You're paying a tax that patriarchy charges women to stay safe and employed. A tax is not a character flaw. You can want to stop paying it and still be someone who sometimes has to.

Kristin Neff's work on self-compassion points in a similar direction: kindness to yourself can be part of change. This jar is not a scorecard.

Room 3 · Tools

Who is looking, and can she look back?

Look again
gaze-gallery.htm—□×

The male gaze. Laura Mulvey (1975) described how images can assume a male viewer and position women as things to be looked at. You already know this; it's the engine of your first objection.

The oppositional gaze. bell hooks argued that looking back can be a political act, especially for Black women whose perspectives have often been excluded. The viewer isn't a bucket the image gets poured into. She can read against the grain, laugh at it, take it apart.

The female gaze. Not simply “pictures women like,” but a way of looking that cares about feeling and being, not just appearing.

Room 4 · Concession

Yes, and she is also a white, thin pop princess. Say it with me.

Add, don't subtract

I'm not going to wriggle out of this one. Kimberlé Crenshaw coined intersectionality to show how systems such as race and gender overlap and shape experiences in ways that can't be understood separately.

Your point stands: the pop-princess slot has historically been handed to women who fit a narrow template, and Sabrina fits it. That is not her fault, and it is also not nothing. Who gets the microphone is a question about the pipeline, not only the person.

Room 5 · Nuance

“It's my choice” isn't the whole story. Neither is “it's all patriarchy.”

With footnotes

You're right about choice feminism. Rosalind Gill and Angela McRobbie describe how empowerment slogans can be bent into marketing: you choose it, so it must be free. The critique is real, and I'm on your side.

Here's the part I want to ask you to hold gently. The same rigour applies to purity. There's no clean place to stand: not in what we wear, what we stream, who we laugh with, or which artists we avoid. Audre Lorde warned that the master's tools won't take down the master's house, and Roxane Gay wrote about being imperfect and still being in it.

Complicity isn't a moral verdict. It's the weather. Everyone's standing in it. What matters is whether we keep asking the questions, and you do.

counter-arguments.txt — pinned, not dismissed—□×

Counter-arguments I take seriously

  • The male gaze might still win. Individual readings don't change how the industry sells the image.
  • “Subversion” can be a story pop tells about itself. Gill and McRobbie would be sceptical, and they have a point.
  • Appropriating the language of empowerment is profitable. Yes.
  • Reading against the grain takes energy not everyone has every day. Also yes. You are allowed to be tired.

These don't cancel the earlier rooms. They sit next to them. That's the point of the word and.

The quiet room · take your time

No colours, no jokes, no widgets. Just this.

If Sabrina's imagery is hard for body-related reasons, that is a complete reason. I'm not going to offer a counter-argument, and nothing in this exhibition is meant to override your body's judgment about what is good for you. You can skip her entirely and be exactly the feminist you already are.

If things ever feel heavier than usual, consider reaching out to a trusted person or a qualified support service in your area. You deserve support without having to justify it.

Honest limits

“Matriarchy” is used here as a design thought experiment and house rule, not a claim that historical matriarchal societies universally existed in a particular form. The strength and limits of oppositional readings of commercial pop are debated. This page simplifies theory for a small, personal exhibition; it is not a substitute for the cited work.

Support information and any current claims should be verified before publication. No lyrics are reproduced. The photo frames load files from an img/ folder next to this page; only use pictures you have the rights to.
